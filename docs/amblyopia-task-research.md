# Additional tasks used in amblyopia research

Reviewed October 28, 2026. This note identifies task families beyond Mangekyo's existing exercises. It is a product research inventory, not a treatment plan or an evaluation of Mangekyo's effectiveness. Research stimuli, optical correction, hardware and supervision can differ materially from a phone adaptation. No study schedule is adopted as a training prescription.

| Task family | What the primary study actually did | Implication for Mangekyo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Position judgments with positional noise** | Seven adults practiced judging the position of a test segment made of Gabor patches relative to a reference. Individual patch positions were jittered, requiring integration of uncertain spatial information. This was a training task, with separate clinical outcome tests. [Primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2765479/). | A useful future extension of Fine alignment: vary spatial uncertainty separately from the mean offset. A static, monocular phone adaptation is feasible as an experimental task, but generic dots or bars would not reproduce the study's calibrated Gabor protocol. |
| **Cued feature counting and selective attention** | Hou and Nicholas trained adults to search for and count targets, preceded by a cue to the trained eye. Targets and distractors were delivered separately to the two eyes. The study included 13 participants with amblyopia and had no invalid-cue comparison, so it could not isolate the cue's contribution. [Primary study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-13747-4). | Counting selected symbols among distractors is a distinct future interaction. A single-screen version would be an experimental monocular attention task; it would not reproduce dichoptic delivery or establish suppression reduction. Eye-separated stimuli would require appropriate equipment and clinical input. |
| **Texture segmentation** | Sireteanu and Rettenbach examined learning in locating a discrepant texture region and visual search, including questions about disturbed early visual experience. The abstract describes adult learning and transfer, but is not sufficient to establish a phone treatment protocol or an amblyopia-specific treatment effect. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8839338/). | A region of differently oriented elements in a field of distractors could add variety. This overlaps the already-considered visual-search family; it should remain a research candidate with its own task metrics. |
| **Temporal discrimination / critical flicker fusion** | Eisen-Enosh and colleagues trained six adults with amblyopia using flickering stimuli and a customized dichoptic system; six normally sighted participants were also studied. The authors describe the small sample and further work needed for clinical application. [Primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10175483/). | Defer a phone implementation: controlled timing, luminance and eye-specific presentation are material parts of this protocol. Mangekyo's avoidance of rapid flashing remains in place. |

## Important distinction: a tested outcome is not necessarily a trained task

A motion task appearing in a paper does not by itself establish evidence for a motion-training game. For example, a study of nine adults trained contrast detection, while sinewave motion detection/discrimination were measured before and after; eight adults served as controls. It did not directly train a random-dot motion-coherence game. [Primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3176008/).

Gabor contrast/orientation tasks, crowded symbols, Vernier-style alignment, visual search, and binocular/stereoscopic approaches were already considered in the [exercise roadmap](exercise-roadmap.md). Fine alignment is implemented in the current change. The candidates in the table above are research directions, not implemented modes.

The most practical next investigations are a positional-noise variant and a counting task, beginning with usability testing. Their task scores would remain separate from professionally measured vision and clinical outcomes. More difficult settings or longer practice are not treated as evidence of improved eyesight.
